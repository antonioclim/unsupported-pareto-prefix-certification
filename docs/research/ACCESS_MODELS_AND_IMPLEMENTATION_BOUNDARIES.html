S4. Access rules, legal simulation and reduction obstructions

S4.1. Whole-row encoding does not preserve prefix certificates

A complete row from a known finite alphabet can be encoded as one integer-valued variable, with its whole pair returned by a public decoder. Such an encoding preserves bundling and a fixed row price. It does not enforce prefix access. The following continuous-triangle example shows that the resulting retrospective certificate can be strictly cheaper.

Let K=N=3K=N=3, with independent missing rows in 𝒯={(c,f):0≤f≤c≤1}\mathcal T=\{(c,f):0\leq f\leq c\leq1\}. The candidate has three identical rows (99/100,299/1000)(99/100,299/1000). The northwest group has rows (0,0),(9/10,9/10),(9/10,9/10)(0,0),(9/10,9/10),(9/10,9/10), and the southeast group has three copies of (1,0)(1,0). All observations have unit cost.

Proposition S7. On this archive, the minimum positive prefix certificate costs eight, whereas the minimum certificate using arbitrary whole-row subsets costs seven.

First, any certificate must reveal all candidate rows. If at most two are revealed, complete its missing rows with (0,0)(0,0) and leave the rivals at their actual values. Under weights (1,1)(1,1), the candidate’s scalar value is at most 2(1289/1000)/3<12(1289/1000)/3<1. The actual rivals have scalar values 6/56/5 and one, so this completion supports the candidate and is FALSE. After candidate census, every southeast row is mandatory: fill its unobserved rows with (0,0)(0,0); with at most two observed rows its mean (r/3,0)(r/3,0) strictly dominates the candidate. Finally, some northwest row equal to (9/10,9/10)(9/10,9/10) must be observed; otherwise all its unknown rows can be zero, again giving domination.

These necessities require at least seven arbitrary whole-row observations. Conversely, reveal the candidate and southeast group completely and only the northwest group’s second row. Its attainable lower and upper corners are (3/10,3/10)(3/10,3/10) and (29/30,29/30)(29/30,29/30). The former has failure 3/10>299/10003/10>299/1000, so it cannot dominate the candidate; the southeast cost one is also safely above the candidate cost. A mixture with northwest weight 61/20061/200 and southeast weight 139/200139/200 gives

Monotonicity under further observations shows that precisely the six northwest subsets containing at least one of its two nonzero rows work, together with the compulsory six candidate and southeast rows.

Under prefix access, obtaining either nonzero northwest row first requires its preceding zero row. Thus at least eight queries are necessary. The prefix (3,2,3)(3,2,3) is sufficient: its northwest lower corner is still (3/10,3/10)(3/10,3/10), while its upper corner decreases to (19/30,19/30)(19/30,19/30). This example concerns the continuous triangle, not either fifteen-symbol alphabet. It defeats immediate equality of the unrestricted-variable and prefix comparators. It does not exclude a more sophisticated reduction or a one-way competitive upper bound from a legally simulated unrestricted policy. Both the legal simulation and its denominator comparison require proof.

The same classification holds analytically on the known five-symbol joint alphabet

𝒮free={(0,0),(1,1),(99/100,299/1000),(9/10,9/10),(1,0)}. \mathcal S_{\mathrm{free}}=\{(0,0),(1,1),(99/100,299/1000),(9/10,9/10),(1,0)\}.

The necessary FALSE completions above use actual rival rows or (0,0)(0,0); the sufficient corner constructions use the legal whole endpoints (0,0)(0,0) and (1,1)(1,1). Thus no continuum assumption is needed for the discrepancy. A single five-valued integer variable can represent each whole row exactly, yet it still omits the prefix prerequisites. This finite-alphabet corollary is analytic; the native checker below enumerates observation states in the continuous triangle rather than every archive over this alphabet.

S4.3. Two obstructions to immediate geometric reductions

The following constructions separate outputs and certificate denominators. They do not rule out reductions that retain additional information.

Pareto membership does not determine GOOD. Work in the square with three configurations and four rows. All four candidate rows are (1/2,1/2)(1/2,1/2). The observed first three northwest rows are (0,5/6)(0,5/6), and the first three southeast rows are (5/6,0)(5/6,0). At prefix (4,3,3)(4,3,3) the feasible mean regions are

{(1/2,1/2)},[0,1/4]×[5/8,7/8],[5/8,7/8]×[0,1/4]. \{(1/2,1/2)\},\qquad [0,1/4]\times[5/8,7/8],\qquad [5/8,7/8]\times[0,1/4].

Every completion has the same three Pareto labels: each rival crosses the candidate, and the two rival rectangles cross each other. Complete both unread rows with (0,0)(0,0). Their means become (0,5/8)(0,5/8) and (5/8,0)(5/8,0), whose equal mixture (5/16,5/16)(5/16,5/16) strictly dominates the candidate. The candidate is therefore unsupported and GOOD holds. Instead complete both unread rows with (1,1)(1,1). The rival coordinate sums are then 9/89/8, whereas the candidate sum is 11, so weights (1,1)(1,1) support the candidate and GOOD fails. A procedure returning only the known Pareto-membership set cannot distinguish these completions. At least one further disclosure is required for GOOD.

S4.4. Boundaries of positive-input policy extension

Positive-only evaluation matters. For Boolean OR on two unit-price bits, compare the full class {0,1}2\{0,1\}^2 with {00,10,11}\{00,10,11\}. Robust TRUE observations coincide. On the smaller class, reading the first bit suffices on every input. On the larger class’s negative input 0000, both bits are necessary. Therefore an extension cannot preserve all input costs, despite preserving positive costs.

Certificate invariance also matters. For predicate x1∧¬x2x_1\land\neg x_2, restricting the full bit-pair class to {00,10}\{00,10\} makes the first bit a cost-one positive certificate on 1010. The full class requires both bits there. These Boolean examples delimit the general argument; they are not additional Pareto results.

Finally, decidable membership is an explicit implementation assumption. The finite alphabets and inequality-defined bands used here satisfy it under exact arithmetic. An arbitrary unspecified domain may require a membership oracle. Correlated rows, different tariffs, informative metadata, changed access rules or noisy observations require separate arguments; endpoint inclusion alone does not establish this extension in those models.

As in main Section 7, a paid-query transcript records the ordered queried locations and their revealed values, excluding the private random tape and internal computation. On a fixed archive there are finitely many such transcripts. Extension between nonnested endpoint-containing domains requires decidable consistency tests for both domains and a shared nonempty positive evaluation set. Certificate equivalence itself permits empty certificates; certificate-normalised ratios require positive denominators. The product-domain Pareto problem has an all-equal negative archive, so an empty transcript cannot certify TRUE.

If each archive is partitioned into public consecutive blocks of identical rows and distinct blocks admit Cartesian-product completions, rounding an observed prefix to the end of its last touched block identifies its block-model certificate with an independent-row certificate. S4.6. Extended comparison with other acquisition models

Priced information and instance complexity. The certificate benchmark follows Charikar et al. (2002), randomised AND/OR evaluation (Laber, 2003, 2008) and the certificate-sensitive analyses of Cicalese and Laber (2011) and Cicalese and Milanič (2008). The remark in main Section 2 identifies it with the input-specific optimum of a globally correct policy, as in deterministic instance complexity (GKN20; LM26). Blanc, Lange and Tan (BLT21) analyse priced Boolean queries under the uniform input distribution, allowing error and comparing expected cost with that of an optimal strategy. Their influence-based guarantee concerns this distributional benchmark; here correctness is exact and cost is normalised by the cheapest realised prefix certificate. Our performance supremum covers positive archives, while correctness covers both answers. Randomised policies retain the robust certificate denominator, unlike bounded-error instance optimality normalised by randomised certificate complexity. On a finite alphabet, each whole row can be treated as one variable (Cicalese & Milanič, 2008), but bit encoding alone does not preserve whole-record costs, legal bit patterns or predecessor payments. Section S4.2 proves an unrestricted-query prefetch comparison with factor Γ(w)=maxg,j∑k≤jwgkwgj, \Gamma(w)=\max_{g,j}\frac{\sum_{k\le j}w_{gk}}{w_{gj}}, which equals NN at unit prices. This comparison accounts for the access difference but does not yield the sharp ratios above. Feder et al. (2003) study interval-constrained inputs whose exact values can be purchased to determine a selection value to prescribed precision. Known prices and adversarial fixed archives also distinguish this model from unknown acquisition costs (Nadimpalli et al., 2025) and stochastic certification under a distribution supported on positive inputs (Ghuge et al., 2026).

Sorted access and certificate discovery. Fagin, Lotem and Naor’s NRA algorithm (FLN03), Section 8.1, uses no random access and may identify the top objects without their exact scores. Its lists are sorted by revealed grades, so the last observed grade constrains every unseen entry. Our archives have arbitrary fixed row orders, and the joint predicate concerns vector means rather than one monotone aggregate. Mandatory predecessors and decisions from incomplete scores therefore have direct precedents, while the geometric verifier and the exact bounds require separate analysis. Blanc et al. (BKLT22) study a complementary certification problem: the input is supplied and queries evaluate an unknown Boolean function; here the predicate is specified and queries disclose the unknown archive.

Ordered certification and partial evaluation. Next-best elicitation already compares prefix-query cost with hindsight evidence for necessarily Pareto-optimal and rank-maximal matchings (HMSS21; Pet22). These problems concern ordinal preference rankings and their legal completions, rather than unsupportedness of a designated vector-mean point. Shah and Yu (2026) compare next-best preference queries with the shortest realised prefix sequence determining all tied voting winners. Their access order and retrospective benchmark are close to ours, while the output is a voting outcome and legal completions are preference rankings. Yu et al. (2026, Theorem 3) recover a conformal calibration quantile from prefix-ordered acceptance verdicts and prove the optimal deterministic ratio 2−1/(n−k+1)2-1/(n-k+1) for finite thresholds when k≥n−k+1k\ge n-k+1, where nn is the calibration sample size and kk the quantile rank. Their certificate and cost statements hold for fixed verdict tables; exchangeability enters the separate coverage guarantee. Sui and Tang (2026) give an exact top-kk certificate for information-disjoint Cartesian loss intervals and a polynomial-time hindsight optimiser for unit-cost empirical Expected Shortfall evaluations. Their exact point queries are freely selectable. They also prove search lower bounds separating online acquisition from hindsight optimisation; the ratios reported for benchmark replays are empirical. The present theorems analyse a designated vector-mean candidate’s joint nondomination and unsupportedness, including exact deterministic and randomised ratios on the unbounded domain, diagonal-band transitions and the candidate-observation obstruction on a fixed alphabet.

Incremental refinement. Tseng and Kirkpatrick (2011) give a direct precedent for prefix revelation through leading bits of numerical inputs and geometric certificate discovery. Their presentation-based denominator differs from the cheapest realised positive prefix here. Gupta et al. (2016) study repeated queries that narrow uncertainty intervals. Our convex row domains fit that state-level pattern: after ss rows in 𝒯d\mathcal T_d, the exact feasible mean region is Lj(s)+(N−s)𝒯d/NL_j(s)+(N-s)\mathcal T_d/N, so successive regions are nested and different-configuration updates commute. The missing ingredients for the present predicate are its verifier, attainable prefix geometry and acquisition bounds. Long uninformative prefixes followed by an informative update are also established lower-bound devices; Meißner (2018, Theorem 4.37) uses them in repeated-update spanning trees. The fixed-alphabet construction here realises a particular ambiguity through legal bounded vector rows and proves its entire TRUE-prefix set.

Geometric retrieval. Bruce et al. (2005) compare maximal-point and convex-hull retrieval with minimum sufficient realised update sets. Charalambous and Hoffmann (2013) prove NP-hardness of minimum verification for unrestricted planar uncertainty regions; coordinate-product regions admit a sharp three-update online bound and polynomial-time minimum verification (Charalambous, 2016, Theorems 6.12 and 6.38, respectively). After reversing the objective order, the closed-rectangle nondomination part of our endpoint criterion is the corresponding maximality test. More recent bounds include Θ(r)\Theta(r) hull retrievals with O(rlog3n)O(r\log^3n) time for constant-complexity polygonal regions (de Berg et al., 2025), and at most 5r5r Pareto-front retrievals for rectangles (de Berg et al., 2026). Here rr is the respective minimum sufficient retrieval count. Those retrievals collapse a whole point region; a query here reveals only its next vector row. Sections S4.1 and S4.3 also give two concrete obstructions to immediate reductions: the same Pareto labels can coexist with both answers to GOOD, and reordering rows while preserving every complete mean can change the prefix comparator from eight to seven.

Search and ordinal reductions. The terminal-choice balancing in main Section 4 is the priced two-box search mechanism (Angelopoulos et al., 2016); its geometric realisation and exclusion of cheaper certificates supply the application here. Bichromatic sorting instead purchases comparisons and certifies an entire order (Goswami & Jacob, 2024), whereas one value disclosure here settles comparisons with every previously disclosed value and a single strict crossing pair suffices. Order-invariant reductions have a long history (Fischer, 2004; Moran et al., 1985). In particular, the causal Ramsey proof adapts the discretised next-action architecture of Baleshzar et al. (2020, Section 3.1). Main Section 3 supplies the additional expected-stopping-cost, legal-continuation and paid-prefix arguments. The cardinal-versus-ordinal online model of Gravin et al. (2023) has an externally supplied arrival order, unlike adaptive choice of the next hidden label here.

Scalarisation and certificate geometry. The limitations of weighted sums for recovering nondominated points are established background (Das & Dennis, 1997). Our convention permits zero coordinates in a nonzero weight vector and counts scalarisation ties as support (Könen & Stiglmayr, 2025). The supporting-weight interval argument behind the two-witness criterion is given in full in Main Lemma 2, including the axis and equality cases. The acquisition results require further analysis of attainable prefix certificates, robust nondomination and the observations needed to discover a certificate under the prescribed access rule. The dd-witness extension uses classical Helly theory; its role here is to bound the number of rival prefixes required in the exact retrospective certificate, with a matching finite example (Section S5.6).

Objective reduction. Preserving dominance under objective omission or approximation is an established subject (BZ09). Low-rank multi-objective linear programming can also reduce the objective dimension by changing the ordering cone (LZ26). These geometric reductions do not by themselves preserve unsupportedness under our axis-weight convention or a paid-prefix observation model. Theorem S16 specialises classical convex geometry to sparse supporting weights and exact scalar feasibility for three alternatives. Theorem S18 supplies the additional public row and completion correspondence needed to preserve the information and comparator costs. Proposition S19 gives sufficient error-margin conditions; Proposition S17 rules out universal compression by nonnegative affine aggregation. No general dimensional-reduction method or new higher-dimensional minimax constant is asserted.
